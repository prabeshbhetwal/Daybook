If Apple built Focus Continuity

A first-principles reconstruction of the design language, interaction grammar and underlying logic Apple would use for a Mac app that records focus sessions, observes presence, and reports honestly on time — derived from Apple's published guidance and from how Apple's own measurement apps actually behave, not from the existing app's design.

How this was researched, and what it is worth

Three inputs. First, Apple's published design guidance, which states its own rules plainly: request permission only when the app clearly needs the data, and only at launch when the app cannot function otherwise; explain the reason in short, specific, sentence-case text; never build a custom screen that imitates a system alert. Second, the observable behaviour of Apple's own measurement products — Screen Time, Health, Sleep, Fitness, Battery, Weather — which are the only real evidence of how Apple designs a product whose entire content is a number it inferred about you. Third, the platform surfaces Apple has built for exactly this shape of app: menu bar extras, Control Center modules, WidgetKit, ActivityKit, App Intents, and the Watch Smart Stack.

The reasoning throughout is: what does Apple do when it faces this exact problem, and what does its own documentation forbid? Where a claim rests on published guidance it is cited. Where it is inference from precedent it says so. Nothing here is derived from the current app's visual design; the existing implementation is referenced only in the delta section, which compares the two on purpose.

One honest caveat: Apple's design pages are rendered client-side and would not serve to a text fetch, so the citations below come from indexed copies of that guidance rather than the live pages. The substance — permission timing, purpose strings, Live Activity interaction rules — is quoted from those copies and is consistent across them.

Part one

The prior question: would Apple ship this as an app at all?

Apple's instinct with a measurement product is to make it a system service with a thin reading surface, not an application you visit. Screen Time is not an app; it is a pane in Settings with a report. Battery health is a pane. Sleep is a mode of the Watch plus a chart inside Health. Fitness rings are a system-wide accounting that a small app merely displays. The pattern is consistent: the operating system does the recording, and the app is a window onto data the system already had.

Applied here, that has one large consequence. Apple would not ask you to declare a focus session with a stopwatch. It already ships a declaration of intent — Focus modes — and it already knows presence, lock, sleep, idle and which app is frontmost. So Apple's version of this product records a session because a Focus is on and you are at the machine. Pressing Start becomes the exception rather than the rule; the default is that turning on Work starts the recording, and turning it off ends it.

The reframe

A focus session is not something you start. It is what the system observed while you were focused. The app's job is to name it, correct it, and remember it.

This is also where the product's name earns its keep. "Continuity" is an Apple word with a specific meaning: one activity, many devices, no seams. An Apple-built Focus Continuity therefore would not be a Mac app that happens to sync. The session would start on the Mac, appear as a Live Activity on the iPhone Lock Screen and in the Watch Smart Stack, be pausable from either, and finish wherever you happened to be. The current app has no story here at all, and it is the single biggest missed opportunity in the brief.

What Apple would refuse to build — the negative brief

A blocking decision
Nothing in an Apple app holds your other actions hostage to a question about data. An unresolved gap is a note, not a gate.
A full-screen takeover
Apple takes the whole screen for a phone call, an alarm, and a security decision. Not for "were you working?"
A word like "evidence"
The app's internal rigour is admirable and its vocabulary is a lab notebook. Apple ships the rigour and hides the notebook.
A score
Apple ships rings, streaks and awards — closed loops with rules you can state — never a composite quality number.
Five peer tabs
Settings is a window opened with ⌘, — never a tab competing with content. That alone removes one of the five.
A custom accent
A Mac app inherits the user's accent colour. Hard-coded blue overrides a preference the person set deliberately.

Part three

Vocabulary

Apple's writing rule is that the interface says what a person would say. The current model is conceptually correct and verbally unshippable: Focused, Focused-active, Tracked, evidence-backed, authoritative, accuracy epoch, integrity qualification. Apple keeps every one of those distinctions and renames all of them.

Current term Apple would say Why
FocusedFocus timeA noun phrase, matching Screen Time's construction, and it names the thing rather than its adjective.
Tracked / At the MacTime on Mac"At the Mac" is already close. "Tracked" implies surveillance; Apple avoids the word entirely outside advertising contexts.
Focused-active(not shown)A necessary internal measure that no user needs named. It should quietly power the goal and the pace sentence and never appear as a label.
Awaiting decisionAway from 1:12 to 2:40 PMApple names the fact, not the app's state machine. The state is visible in what the sentence says.

Sentence-level voice: second person, present tense, no exclamation, no encouragement. "You focused for 2 hours 25 minutes today." Not "Great work — 2h 25m focused!" When there is nothing to say, Apple says nothing rather than filling the space.

Part four

Information architecture

The current app puts five peer tabs in a centred capsule rail. Two things are wrong with that on a Mac. A centred segmented rail as primary navigation is an iOS/iPadOS shape; a Mac app of this kind uses a source list on the left and puts mode switching in the toolbar. And Settings is not a tab — a Mac app has a Settings window, opened with ⌘, from the app menu, and putting preferences inside the content area breaks a convention every Mac user relies on without exception.

Current
Five peer tabs
Focus · Today · Review · Insights · Settings. Focus is the live surface; Review contains Week, Month and History as a sub-control; Insights is gated and often empty; Settings is eight groups in a two-pane layout inside the tab.
Apple's version
A sidebar of periods, one canvas
Sidebar: Today, This Week, This Month, then a History section listing recent months, then Awards. Toolbar carries the live session control and a Day/Week/Month scope where relevant. Settings moves to its own window. Insights dissolves into the surfaces it describes.

Where each of the five tabs goes

Focus
Becomes the menu bar item and the toolbar. A live session is ambient, not a destination — Apple would not make you open a window to see a clock that could sit in the menu bar. The window's Today view shows the running session as the first row of the day, live.
Today
Stays, as the default sidebar selection. Gains the live session at the top and the day's timeline as its one dominant graphic — a decision the existing design already got right.
Review
Splits into sidebar items. This Week and This Month become peers; History becomes the sidebar's second section rather than a third chart mode. Selecting a bar selects that day in place, with an Open in Today affordance — as the current design already specifies.
Insights
Removed as a tab. Apple does not build a room for conclusions. Pace belongs under the goal ring in Today; rhythm belongs as a second chart in Week; continuity belongs as a streak line in Awards. A tab that is often empty by design is a tab that teaches people not to open it.
Settings
Its own window, ⌘, , with a top tab bar of at most five panes — General, Sessions, Away & Breaks, Recording, Privacy. Eight groups is two too many; Appearance is a system preference, and Advanced diagnostics belong behind an Option-click or in the Help menu.
Part five

Visual language

The governing principle is deference: the app is a window onto content, and its chrome should be made of the system's own materials so that it changes when the system changes. A Mac app that ships its own colour tokens for surfaces is, in Apple's terms, fighting the platform.

The one place a bespoke palette is legitimate is the category ramp for work types and apps, because those colours are data keys and must stay stable across every chart. Apple does exactly this in Screen Time. Keep the existing seven-colour ramp; derive it from the system colour set so it survives Increase Contrast, and pair every colour with a label so nothing is encoded by hue alone.

Type

San Francisco, as text styles rather than point sizes: .largeTitle for the day's headline figure, .title2 for view titles, .headline for group headers, .body for rows, .footnote for qualification. Named styles are what let Dynamic Type and Increase Contrast work; a 26 pt literal does not scale.

SF Rounded for the live timer is a defensible choice — it is what Timer and Fitness use for a running figure — but Apple would apply it only to the clock and never to metric values in tables, where rounded digits read as decorative. All times use .monospacedDigit() so numbers do not jitter as they tick.

Density and metrics
Mac row heights of 24–28 pt for list rows, not 44–52. The existing 52 pt row is an iOS touch metric; on a Mac it makes a day of app usage into a scroll. Apple's own Screen Time app list is dense.

8 pt spacing rhythm: correct, keep it. Corner radius: match the system's control radius rather than 16/12 pt panels — most groupings need a hairline separator, not a card.

Icon and symbols

SF Symbols throughout, hierarchical rendering, which the current design already specifies. The menu bar glyph must be a template image that inverts with the menu bar's appearance, legible at 16 pt, and distinguishable at a glance between four states — off, running, paused, needs attention — by shape, since the menu bar is monochrome. App icon: a single object on a rounded-rectangle ground, rendered for light, dark and tinted appearances.

Part six

Surfaces

Apple's guidance for the menu bar is that a menu bar extra provides quick access to a frequently used function without opening the app; on macOS 26 these items also sit above windows and receive a slight vibrancy behind them. The design consequence is that the ambient surface should be small, decisive and mostly text.

Surface Contents and rules
Menu bar itemGlyph always; elapsed time only while running and only if the person turns it on — a permanently changing number in the menu bar is a distraction, and this is an app about not being distracted. Four glyph states, differentiated by shape.
Menu (click)Apple would prefer a real menu over a custom popover: a live header row, one primary command, up to three recent sessions to resume, break status as a disabled informational item, then Open Focus Continuity · Settings… · Quit. A popover is justified only by the goal ring; if the ring goes into the header as a 14 pt inline gauge, the menu wins on speed, keyboard access and system fidelity.
Control Center moduleA start/stop control, so the app is reachable from the same place as Focus itself. This is the most Apple-native addition available and it costs one ControlWidget.
WidgetsSmall: today's ring plus focus total. Medium: seven-day bars. Large: today's timeline. Lock Screen circular and inline on iPhone. Widgets are a timeline of pre-rendered views, so they show the last known figure with its time, never a spinner.
Live ActivityThe continuity payoff. Compact: glyph + elapsed. Expanded: session name, elapsed, goal progress. Minimal: glyph. Apple's rules are explicit — no buttons and nothing that looks like a button, because Lock Screen and Dynamic Island presentations are not interactive, and every tap opens the app; and the system keeps a finished activity on the Lock Screen for up to four hours unless you dismiss it sooner. A finished session should dismiss after about fifteen minutes, long enough to read the total.
Watch Smart StackThe same Live Activity appears at the top of the Smart Stack on a paired Watch; Apple recommends a custom layout for the wrist rather than accepting the default. One glyph, elapsed time, and the session name truncated to one line.
App IntentsStart focus session, Pause, End, Get today's focus time — exposed to Siri, Shortcuts, Spotlight and Automations. This is how a Focus mode turning on triggers a session without the app watching anything. The current app has none of this and it is the cheapest large win in the report.
Part seven

Charts, and how Apple states uncertainty

Apple's charting guidance opens on the point that a chart exists to communicate with clarity — which in practice means one measure per chart, a legible axis, and no ornament. Swift Charts is the implementation, and its defaults are the design: bars with system fills, sparse axis marks, a rule mark for the average, and selection by click or drag with a value callout.

The current design's strongest call is already Apple-correct: tracked time in the bars, work-type composition kept separate, because composition is a different measure and stacking it would silently redefine the axis. Keep that. Two amendments.

First, draw the gaps. A day with four hours recorded and two hours unmeasured should show the unmeasured portion as a hatched or quaternary-fill remainder rather than a shorter bar, because a shorter bar is indistinguishable from a shorter day. This is the Sleep chart's move and it converts an integrity footnote into a picture.

Second, qualify at the bottom, once. One footnote line under the chart — "Some time before 12 August is not recorded. About this data" — replaces per-row amber icons. The link opens a short plain-language sheet: what is measured, what is not, where the file lives.

Every chart needs its table. Apple's accessibility expectation for a chart is that it carries an equivalent summary a screen reader can read — an audio-graph-style description plus the underlying values. The current design already requires this; it should be a real, visible Show as table affordance, not only an accessibility label.

The honesty doctrine, in Apple's terms
Absence is drawn, not written
Missing time is a visible hole in the graphic. Zero is a measurement; nothing is not.
The default is the cautious reading
An unresolved gap is not counted until you say otherwise. The app never asks permission to be careful.
One sentence, then a link
Explanation is available, never mandatory reading. No warning colour on a data condition.
Part eight

Interruption etiquette

This is the area where the current app diverges from Apple most sharply, and the divergence is structural rather than cosmetic. Today, an extended absence creates a decision, and while that decision is unresolved, start, stop, pause, continue and the global hotkey are all blocked and routed back to it. Absences over thirty minutes are asked about on a blurred full-screen prompt.

Apple would not ship either behaviour. The whole screen is reserved for things that cannot wait — an incoming call, an alarm, a security prompt. And nothing in an Apple app disables unrelated commands to force an answer about historical data. The rule is: the app decides conservatively, tells you what it did, and lets you change it whenever you like.

Now
Blurred full-screen prompt · four actions · everything else blocked until answered · hotkey routed back to the prompt
Apple
Session already ended at the gap, not counted · one notification with two actions · a correction row at the top of Today that stays until dismissed · nothing blocked, ever

The notification rules that apply

Never time-sensitive
A question about the past is not urgent, so it must not break through a Focus mode. Standard interruption level, delivered quietly, batched into the scheduled summary if the person uses one.
Never during a session
An app that interrupts focus to talk about focus has failed at its own premise. Break reminders are the single exception, and they should be delivered as a quiet banner the person opted into, with a length they chose.
Actions on the notification
Two, maximum: Count as focus and Not focus. The four-way choice — continue, merge, break, reset — is a state-machine menu; two of those four are the same arithmetic with a different label and should be one action plus an optional "call it a break" in the app.
Rewards
Not notifications at all. Fitness awards are discovered, not announced. Move them to the Awards sidebar item and remove the HUD entirely.
Part nine

First run, and the privacy story

Onboarding

One sheet on first launch. A large glyph, a title, three short lines describing what the app records, and Continue. No carousel, no permission gauntlet, no sample data. Apple's pattern is to defer teaching to the moment it is needed, so the empty states carry the rest: each is an SF Symbol, a one-line title and one sentence — and the Today empty state keeps the timeline scaffold visible so the shape of the answer is already on screen.

Two setup decisions are worth asking at first run because they change what the app does rather than how it looks: a daily goal, and whether sessions should start automatically with a Focus mode. Both get sensible defaults and both live in Settings afterwards. Everything else — density, timeline labels, appearance — is a preference nobody should be asked about before they have seen a single number.

Apple would also add a Set up on iPhone step, because Continuity is the promise in the name, and would use the standard device-handoff sheet rather than instructions.

Privacy

The app's position here is already stronger than most: no Accessibility, Automation, Screen Recording or Input Monitoring permission is requested, idle and input density come from system counters rather than event content, and everything is stored locally. Apple's guidance is to request permission only when the app clearly needs it, and only at launch when the app cannot function otherwise; the ideal is to need none. This app meets the ideal.

So Apple would state it, once, where it matters: a short line in the onboarding sheet — "Recording happens on this Mac. Nothing is sent anywhere." — and a Privacy pane in Settings that names exactly what is stored (app names and time ranges, never content), shows the folder, and offers deletion with an explicit scope.

What Apple would not do is dress this up. No shield iconography, no "privacy first" badge, no pre-alert screen imitating a system dialog — the guidance explicitly forbids mirroring the system alert's appearance or its Allow button. If sync arrives, the framing changes to end-to-end encrypted iCloud and the same sentence gets one more clause.

Motion

Apple's motion budget for a utility of this kind is almost nothing, and the current design's instinct — motion communicates a state or data transition only — is right. In practice that means four animations in the whole app: a numeric content transition on the live timer, a spring on the goal ring's fill, the standard selection transition in the sidebar, and the sheet presentation. Everything else is instant. Reduce Motion replaces the ring spring and the sheet with cross-fades. No entrance animation on charts: a bar chart that grows from zero every time you open a window is a chart you have to wait for.

Part eleven

The logic behind it

The existing engine is genuinely good: an exhaustive state machine, a presence gate that knows a wake resets the idle counter, work spread across a session's span rather than filed under the day it ended. Apple's version keeps all of that. What changes is the shape of the model: fewer declared states, more derived ones, and a strict separation between what was observed and what it was interpreted as.

Two layers, never mixed

Layer 1 — Observations (append-only)
Immutable, timestamped, never rewritten: frontmost-app intervals, idle samples, lock, sleep, wake, display state, Focus mode changes. This is the app's ledger. Corrections never touch it.
Layer 2 — Interpretations (rebuildable)
Sessions, breaks, away spans, focus totals, goal progress. Derived by a pure function from Layer 1 plus a small set of user corrections. Deleting the derived store loses nothing.

This is how Health works, and it dissolves the app's hardest current problem. The "accuracy epoch" exists because the recorder changed and old derived data could not be trusted. With a rebuildable interpretation layer, a recorder fix means replaying the ledger under the new rules — the ledger was always accurate; only the interpretation was wrong. Where the ledger itself is genuinely thin, the day shows a hole, which is the truth and needs no epoch vocabulary.

The state machine, reduced

Seven pause reasons and four user decisions is a rich vocabulary for a small question. Apple's cut: three states and one correction.

enum SessionState { case none, active, suspended(Suspension) }

enum Suspension {           // why the clock is not running
  case byPerson             // you pressed pause, or turned the Focus off
  case notPresent           // idle, locked, asleep — nobody is here
  case presentNotWorking    // watching, or a break app in front
}

struct Correction: Codable {   // the only user-writable interpretation
  let span: DateInterval
  let verdict: Verdict         // .focus(WorkType) | .rest | .notCounted
  let madeAt: Date
}

The distinctions the current model draws — sleep versus lock versus idle, away versus break — do not disappear; they move into the observation ledger where they belong as facts, and out of the state machine where they were behaviours. Everything a person can say about a span reduces to one of three verdicts, which is why the notification needs two actions rather than four.

Behavioural rules

Never block
No unresolved state gates any command, hotkey or menu item. Ever. An open question is a row, not a lock.
Default conservative
Unattributed time is not focus. The app never asks for permission to under-count, only for permission to count more.
Correct forever
Any span is re-attributable for as long as its record exists, from the row it appears in. There is no window during which a correction is possible and after which it is not.
Automatic is visible
A session the app started says so, in its row, and is as deletable as one you started. The asymmetric start/stop thresholds that prevent flapping stay — that reasoning is already correct.
Sleep is not away
A closed lid ends the session at the lid, silently, with no question on return. There is nothing to ask about: the machine recorded the fact.
Midnight
Keep the existing per-day clipping. Apple's calendars clip too, and the alternative — filing a session under the day it ended — is the bug the current code already documents.

Data model and API sketch

// ── Layer 1: the ledger ────────────────────────────────────────────
struct Observation: Codable, Identifiable {   // append-only, per device
  let id: UUID
  let interval: DateInterval
  let kind: Kind
  enum Kind: Codable {
    case app(bundleID: String, name: String)
    case idle(seconds: TimeInterval)
    case locked, asleep, displayOff
    case focusMode(identifier: String, on: Bool)
    case inputDensity(keysPerMinute: Double, clicksPerMinute: Double)
  }
}

// ── Layer 2: what it means ─────────────────────────────────────────
struct FocusSession: Codable, Identifiable {
  let id: UUID
  var title: String?              // nil until named; never "Untitled"
  var workType: WorkType
  var interval: DateInterval      // wall clock, start to end
  var focusTime: TimeInterval     // clock time, suspensions removed
  var origin: Origin              // .person | .focusMode(String) | .detected
  var deviceOrigin: DeviceKind    // mac | iPhone | iPad
  var groupID: UUID               // segments of one piece of work
}

// ── The one pure function everything reads through ─────────────────
func interpret(_ observations: [Observation],
               corrections: [Correction],
               rules: Rules,
               calendar: Calendar) -> Interpretation
// Interpretation: sessions, breaks, awaySpans, unmeasured, dayTotals.
// Deterministic. No I/O. Replayable. The whole test surface.

// ── Reading surfaces ──────────────────────────────────────────────
protocol FocusStore {                            // the app's only API
  func day(_ date: Date) async -> DayReport
  func period(_ range: DateInterval, by: Granularity) async -> PeriodReport
  var live: AsyncStream<LiveSession?> { get }    // menu bar, widget, LA
  func start(title: String?, type: WorkType) async throws
  func suspend(_ reason: Suspension) async
  func end() async
  func correct(_ correction: Correction) async
}
Persistence. SwiftData or a Core Data store rather than two hand-rolled JSON envelopes, because the ledger will be large and needs indexed range queries, and because migration becomes a schema concern rather than a versioned-envelope concern. The atomic-write discipline in the current code is right; the format is what limits it.

Sync. CloudKit private database, one zone, ledger records append-only so conflict resolution is trivial — appends never collide. Corrections carry madeAt and last-writer-wins is acceptable because a correction is an opinion. Interpretation is per-device and never synced.
Live surfaces. One AsyncStream of the live session drives the menu bar, the widget timeline reload, the Live Activity update and the Watch. The single one-second ticker in the current design stays, as the stream's source; nothing else in the app owns a timer.

Intents. Every verb above is an AppIntent, which buys Siri, Shortcuts, Spotlight, the Control Center control and Focus-mode automation from one definition. This is the mechanism by which "turning on Work starts a session" needs no new permission and no polling.

The kill list

The Insights tab. Its four sections have better homes. A tab that gates itself teaches people not to open it.
The reward HUD. Awards are discovered in a list, never announced over your work.
Two of the four away decisions. "Away" and "break" differ in what is written down, not in the arithmetic — so one action plus an optional relabel.
Interface-density and timeline-label preferences. Pick the right density and ship it. A setting here is an unmade decision.
The Appearance group. Follow the system. An in-app light/dark override contradicts a preference the person already set.
"Advanced" as a visible group. Diagnostics belong behind Option-click or in Help. Destructive actions keep their confirmation and their isolation.

What the current app already does the way Apple would

Worth naming, because it is most of the hard part. Spreading a session's work across its span rather than filing it under the day it ended. Refusing to rewrite preserved history. Keeping composition out of the tracked-time bars. Requesting no privacy permission at all and using system counters rather than event content. One ticker for the whole app. Not counting rest as focus. A settings surface with no non-functional controls. Asymmetric auto-start and auto-stop thresholds so an automatic mode does not flap. Naming Watching as presence rather than absence. Each of those is a decision Apple would make and most products get wrong.
